How it works

1. Newton's law of gravity

Every body pulls on every other body. The pull from a body of mass M at distance r gives an acceleration of a = G·M / r², pointing toward it. Each body adds up the pulls from all the others. We visit each pair only once and apply equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law), which halves the work.

2. Stepping through time

A computer can't run time continuously, so we take tiny steps of length dt.

Euler moves each body using its current velocity, then updates the velocity. It's simple, but every step adds a little energy, so orbits slowly spiral outward.

Velocity Verlet does a half-step "kick" to the velocity, moves ("drift"), recomputes gravity, then does the second half-kick. It's symplectic: it preserves the geometry of the motion, so energy errors stay tiny and don't grow. Switch integrators and watch the energy graph.

3. Energy

Kinetic energy is ½mv² and gravitational potential energy is −G·m₁m₂ / r. With nothing outside the system, their sum should stay constant. The white line on the graph is that total. A flat line means the simulation is trustworthy.

4. Softening

Real gravity becomes infinite when two bodies touch (r → 0). We use r² + ε² with a small ε so very close passes don't explode.

5. Collisions

When two bodies touch they merge. Mass and momentum are conserved exactly, but some kinetic energy turns into heat, so the total energy line jumps (marked with a dashed line). The amount lost is ½·μ·v², where v is their relative speed and μ = m₁m₂ / (m₁ + m₂). The drift readout allows for exactly that loss, so it only counts real numerical error. To find bodies quickly, collisions use "sweep and prune": bodies are sorted left to right, and each one only checks neighbours that overlap it horizontally.

6. The gravity field

The "rubber sheet" shows how deep the gravitational well (the potential, −G·M / r) is at each point of the middle plane. Heavy bodies sit over deep funnels. A body released at rest rolls straight downhill, and an orbit is like a marble circling round a funnel. Gravity has no edge; it reaches forever and just gets weaker, so the sheet flattens out smoothly instead of stopping. The depth uses a logarithmic scale, otherwise stars' wells would be too deep to see anything else.

7. Barnes-Hut: thousands of bodies

Checking every pair gets slow fast: 1,500 bodies means over a million pairs, every step. The Barnes-Hut method (1986) puts space in a cube, then splits crowded cubes into eight smaller ones, building an octree.

A distant cluster pulls almost exactly like one body with the cluster's total mass at its centre of mass. So if a cube is small compared to its distance (size / distance < θ), its whole contents count as one pull. Nearby bodies are still calculated one by one. The work grows like n log n instead of n².

The catch: it's an approximation. Turn on "Show Barnes-Hut tree", select a body, and move the θ slider to see the trade-off between speed and accuracy. The energy graph shows the small extra error compared to the exact method.

8. Colliding galaxies

Each galaxy is a black hole plus 1,500 "stars" (each one standing for millions of real stars) on circular orbits. Real stars are so far apart that they practically never hit each other, so here they pass straight through each other: a collisionless system. Only gravity shapes it.

Tidal tails form because gravity weakens with distance: the other galaxy pulls its near side harder than its centre, and its centre harder than its far side, stretching it out. A galaxy spinning the same way as the encounter (prograde) stays lined up with that stretch for longer, so its outer stars get flung into long tails. One spinning the other way (retrograde) barely changes. Alar and Juri Toomre showed this with a simple computer model in 1972; it explained the strange shapes of real galaxies like the Antennae.

Galaxy centres aren't points: the black hole sits in a dense ball of stars. So this scenario uses more softening (ε = 15), which also keeps the energy error around 0.1% instead of 5%.

9. Asteroids, comets and neutron stars

Gravity treats them all the same; only their mass and size differ. A comet's tail always points away from the nearest star, not behind it: sunlight and the solar wind push gas and dust off the comet. The tail grows as the comet gets closer and hotter. A neutron star is the collapsed core of a giant star: twice the Sun's mass squeezed into a ball the size of a city. From far away it pulls exactly like a normal star of the same mass. You can only tell the difference up close.

10. The Equations tab and Kepler's laws

Select a body and open the Equations tab to see each formula with the simulation's live numbers plugged in. The orbit card treats the body and the one it orbits as a pair on their own (the "two-body problem"), which has an exact answer: an ellipse with the heavier body at one focus (Kepler's 1st law). The body moves fastest when it's closest (2nd law), and the time for one lap grows like T² ∝ a³ (3rd law). The dashed ellipse on the canvas is that prediction. The other bodies pull on it too, so the real path slowly drifts away from the ellipse.

11. Three dimensions

Everything works the same in 3D: each position, velocity and force just has a third part, z (height above the middle plane). Real orbits are tilted a little, so the Solar System planets go slightly above and below the grid. An orbit always stays flat in its own plane, because angular momentum (r × v) is conserved; the tilted galaxies show what happens when two such planes meet.

The picture is drawn with Three.js. Our code keeps the camera position (how far away, how far round, how high up), and Three.js turns the 3D scene into pixels on the graphics card. Observe (Esc) only looks around, so you can't move a body by accident; Move (M) drags bodies.
