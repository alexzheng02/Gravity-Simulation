How it works

1. Newton's law of gravity

Every body pulls on every other body. The pull from a body of mass M at distance r gives an acceleration of a = G·M / r², pointing toward it. Each body adds up the pulls from all the others. We visit each pair only once and apply equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law), which halves the work.

2. Stepping through time

A computer can't run time continuously, so we take tiny steps of length dt.

Euler moves each body using its current velocity, then updates the velocity. It's simple, but every step adds a little energy, so orbits slowly spiral outward.

Velocity Verlet does a half-step "kick" to the velocity, moves ("drift"), recomputes gravity, then does the second half-kick. It's symplectic: it preserves the geometry of the motion, so energy errors stay tiny and don't grow. Switch integrators and watch the energy graph.

3. Energy

Kinetic energy is ½mv² and gravitational potential energy is −G·m₁m₂ / r. With nothing outside the system, their sum should stay constant. The white line on the graph is that total. A flat line means the simulation is trustworthy.

4. Softening

Real gravity becomes infinite when two bodies touch (r → 0). We use r² + ε² with a small ε so very close passes don't explode.

5. Collisions

When two bodies touch they merge. Mass and momentum are conserved exactly, but some kinetic energy turns into heat, so the total energy line jumps (marked with a dashed line). The amount lost is ½·μ·v², where v is their relative speed and μ = m₁m₂ / (m₁ + m₂). The drift readout allows for exactly that loss, so it only counts real numerical error. To find bodies quickly, collisions use "sweep and prune": bodies are sorted left to right, and each one only checks neighbours that overlap it horizontally.

6. The gravity field

Gravity's "well" (the potential, −G·M / r) fills all of space. In 3D, every body is wrapped in nested shells of equal potential, like the layers of an onion. Drawing every shell would hide everything, so we show a slice through space at the grid's height. Move it up and down (Q / Z) to scan through the field.

Contour map: bright means a deep well, and the rings are lines of equal potential. Around a single star they're circles, the slice through its spherical shells. A body released at rest falls straight across them, downhill. Rubber sheet: the same slice, pushed down by the well's depth. An orbit is like a marble circling round a funnel. But the sheet's "down" isn't a real direction: it shows how deep the well is, not which way gravity pulls. Pull sheet fixes that: the same depth, but each point is pushed the way gravity really pulls there. Slice above a planet and it sags down to it; below, it bulges up to it; level with it, the lines are drawn in sideways. Gravity field arrows show the pull all around at once: each arrow points along g, the pull per unit of mass, longer and brighter where it's stronger. Gravity has no edge; it reaches forever and just gets weaker, so both fade out smoothly instead of stopping. Depth uses a logarithmic scale, otherwise stars' wells would be too deep to see anything else.

7. Barnes-Hut: thousands of bodies

Checking every pair gets slow fast: 1,500 bodies means over a million pairs, every step. The Barnes-Hut method (1986) puts space in a cube, then splits crowded cubes into eight smaller ones, building an octree.

A distant cluster pulls almost exactly like one body with the cluster's total mass at its centre of mass. So if a cube is small compared to its distance (size / distance < θ), its whole contents count as one pull. Nearby bodies are still calculated one by one. The work grows like n log n instead of n².

The catch: it's an approximation. Turn on "Barnes-Hut tree" (⚙ Options), select a body, and move the θ slider to see the trade-off between speed and accuracy. The energy graph shows the small extra error compared to the exact method.

8. Colliding galaxies

Each galaxy is a black hole plus 1,500 "stars" (each one standing for millions of real stars) on circular orbits. Real stars are so far apart that they practically never hit each other, so here they pass straight through each other: a collisionless system. Only gravity shapes it.

Tidal tails form because gravity weakens with distance: the other galaxy pulls its near side harder than its centre, and its centre harder than its far side, stretching it out. A galaxy spinning the same way as the encounter (prograde) stays lined up with that stretch for longer, so its outer stars get flung into long tails. One spinning the other way (retrograde) barely changes. Alar and Juri Toomre showed this with a simple computer model in 1972; it explained the strange shapes of real galaxies like the Antennae.

Galaxy centres aren't points: the black hole sits in a dense ball of stars. So this scenario uses more softening (ε = 15), which also keeps the energy error around 0.1% instead of 5%.

9. Asteroids, comets and neutron stars

Gravity treats them all the same; only their mass and size differ. A comet's tail always points away from the nearest star, not behind it: sunlight and the solar wind push gas and dust off the comet. The tail grows as the comet gets closer and hotter. A neutron star is the collapsed core of a giant star: about 1.4 times the Sun's mass squeezed into a ball the size of a city. From far away it pulls exactly like a normal star of the same mass. You can only tell the difference up close.

10. The Equations tab and Kepler's laws

Select a body and open the Equations tab to see each formula with the simulation's live numbers plugged in. The orbit card treats the body and the one it orbits as a pair on their own (the "two-body problem"), which has an exact answer: an ellipse with the heavier body at one focus (Kepler's 1st law). The body moves fastest when it's closest (2nd law), and the time for one lap grows like T² ∝ a³ (3rd law). The dashed ellipse on the canvas is that prediction. The other bodies pull on it too, so the real path slowly drifts away from the ellipse.

11. Three dimensions

Everything works the same in 3D: each position, velocity and force just has a third part, z (height above the middle plane). Real orbits are tilted a little, so the Solar System planets go slightly above and below the grid. An orbit always stays flat in its own plane, because angular momentum (r × v) is conserved; the tilted galaxies show what happens when two such planes meet.

Misclicked? Undo (Ctrl+Z) puts the system back to just before your last change, rewinding time to that moment. Redo (Ctrl+Shift+Z) goes forward again.

The picture is drawn with Three.js. Our code keeps the camera position (how far away, how far round, how high up), and Three.js turns the 3D scene into pixels on the graphics card. Observe (Esc) only looks around, so you can't move a body by accident; Move (M) drags bodies. With "Auto-pause while editing" on, the simulation waits while you place, drag or edit a body. With it off, everything keeps moving; a body you drag is held still under the mouse (still pulling on the others) and carries on with its old velocity when you let go.

The grid is the working plane: a flat plane at the height the camera looks at. New bodies are placed on it, the gravity field is sliced along it, and the thin lines from each body show how far above or below it they are. Move the view with right-drag, W A S D, Q / Z (up and down) or the buttons on the canvas; Fit View (F) or following a body also sets its height.

12. How we checked the physics

34 automated tests compare the simulator with answers worked out independently: Kepler's laws, escape speed, conservation of energy and momentum, collisions, the known figure-8 solution, and Barnes-Hut against the exact method. All pass. The details are in VALIDATION.md, and anyone can rerun them with node tests/validate.js.

Your field guide

A little help
exploring the stars.

Pick a topic below. Pause whenever you want time to experiment. Your simulation stays here while you read.

01 Start, pause & choose a scenario
  1. Choose a preset from Scenario at the top. Empty Space lets you build from scratch.
  2. Use Pause / Play or Space. The Speed slider changes how fast simulation time passes.
  3. Reset or R reloads the current preset. Undo can restore the system before a reset or scenario change.

First visit? Try Star & Planet, then Solar System.

02 Add a body or throw one
  1. Choose Planet, Star, Moon, Black Hole, Asteroid, Comet, or Neutron Star beneath the canvas (keys 1–7).
  2. Click empty space on the grid for an automatic orbit. Hold briefly before releasing to read the target and see the predicted path.
  3. Drag and release to throw instead: direction follows the drag; a longer drag gives more speed. The dashed path previews the launch and a red × warns of a predicted collision.
  4. Press Esc or choose Observe to stop placing. Clicking an existing body selects it.

Adding a moon: click near its planet, outside the surface. Check that the preview names the planet. A stellar-tides warning means the moon may escape or crash; snapping does not guarantee stability.

New bodies affect the whole system. Existing bodies keep their velocities, so adding mass can cause real instability. A massive body without a suitable host inherits the system’s average motion.

03 Look around & follow a body
  • Observe: drag to rotate; scroll or pinch to zoom. On touch screens, use two fingers to pan.
  • Pan: right-drag, Shift + drag in Observe, or W A S D. Arrow keys rotate.
  • Height: Q / Z raises / lowers the view and placement grid. New bodies start on that grid.
  • Fit View (F) brings everything into view. Top View (T) toggles the overhead view.
  • Camera: follow the center of mass, the selected body, or a named body. Choose Free to explore independently.
  • Open View controls on the canvas for on-screen movement, rotation, height, and zoom buttons. Hold a button to keep moving.
04 Select, move, resize & delete
  1. Click a body on the canvas or in Controls → Bodies. Its properties appear under Selected Body.
  2. Change Mass or Radius with sliders or number boxes. Mass changes gravity; radius changes visible size and the collision boundary.
  3. Edit Position X / Y / Z, or choose Move (M) and drag the body. Shift + drag moves it vertically.
  4. Use Delete in the inspector or the Delete key to remove the selected body.

The Move tool holds the body while you drag, then releases it with its previous velocity. It still attracts other bodies while held.

05 Change speed & direction
  1. Select the body, then open Controls → Selected Body.
  2. Edit Velocity X, Y, Z. X and Y are motion across the grid; Z is vertical motion. Negative values reverse an axis’s direction.
  3. Type a number and press Enter or leave the field to apply it. The Speed readout shows the combined magnitude.

Set all three velocity components to 0 to stop it momentarily. Gravity immediately accelerates it again when the simulation runs. The global Speed slider changes time, not a body’s velocity.

06 Make an existing body orbit
  1. Select the body you want to orbit. Place or move it outside its intended host’s surface.
  2. In Selected Body → Orbit around, choose the host explicitly. Select Clockwise or Anticlockwise.
  3. Click Make it orbit. This replaces the selected body’s velocity with a circular-orbit starting velocity relative to that host.

For a moon, choose the planet—not the star. Other bodies can still disturb the orbit. Make it orbit does not move the body or lock its path.

07 Undo, redo & edit while paused
  • Undo: button or Ctrl/⌘ Z. Redo: button or Ctrl/⌘ Shift Z.
  • Undo rewinds to before an edit, including placements, deletions, resets, and scenario changes.
  • In Options, enable Auto-pause while editing to pause during placement, movement, sliders, and typing. It resumes afterward unless you paused manually.
  • Clear Trails clears the drawn motion history without changing the bodies.
08 Show gravity, trails & overlays

Open Options at the top to toggle:

  • Trails / Labels: past motion and body names.
  • Force & velocity arrows: acceleration in orange, velocity in green.
  • Gravity field arrows (3D): the direction gravity pulls throughout space.
  • Predicted orbit: a two-body Kepler ellipse for the selected body and its reference host.
  • Barnes-Hut tree: the spatial groups used by the fast solver.
  • Reference grid / Background stars / Slowly turn the view / Performance readout: scene and display preferences.
  • Collisions merge: touching bodies merge when enabled; disable it to let bodies pass through one another.

Under Controls → Physics → Gravity field, choose Contour map (potential glow and rings), Rubber sheet (well depth), or Pull sheet (pull direction). The slice follows the grid’s height; use Q / Z to scan it. Choose Off to hide it.

Options are saved in this browser. Use Reset to defaults inside Options to restore them, or Done to close the menu.

09 Read equations & energy
  • Select a body, then open Equations (E) or use See the equations for this body.
  • Compared with picks the reference body. This is shared with the inspector’s Orbit around menu.
  • Read live force, acceleration, kinetic and potential energy, momentum, escape speed, and Kepler orbit values. Numbers use simulation units with G = 1.
  • Under Controls → Energy, blue is kinetic, red is potential, and white is total energy. Drift estimates numerical error; edits reset its baseline and collision losses are accounted for.

A predicted ellipse assumes an isolated pair. In a system with several bodies, the actual path can differ.

10 Choose a solver & learn more
  • Integrator: Velocity Verlet is the usual stable choice. Euler demonstrates numerical drift and can cause orbits to spiral outward.
  • Gravity solver: Direct calculates every pair; Barnes-Hut approximates distant groups. Auto switches to Barnes-Hut above 200 bodies.
  • Barnes-Hut accuracy θ: lower is more accurate and slower; higher is faster and more approximate. The solver readout reports work and speedup.
  • Open How it works at the bottom of Controls for the equations, algorithms, approximations, and validation.
  • Click section headings to fold Controls sections, or the Scenario / View controls tabs to fold canvas overlays. Find the team and story under About us in the footer.
